Local Rank Tracking: How to Read Your Google Maps Positions

Your ranking is not one number. It changes with where the customer stands. This guide explains what local rank tracking measures, how to read a grid, and where the numbers mislead.

Why "what is my rank?" has no single answer

Ask three friends in different parts of your city to search "salon near me" and you will likely get three different lists. Google's local results are built mainly from relevance (how well your profile matches the search), distance (how far you are from the searcher or the place they named) and prominence (how well known you are, including reviews and web presence). Distance is measured from the searcher, so the answer to "where do I rank?" is always "for whom, and from where?"

That is why a shop owner who checks Google from his own counter often sees himself in the top three, while a customer two kilometres away never sees the shop at all. Owners are the worst judges of their own ranking because they search from the best possible spot, usually with a history of visiting their own profile.

What a local grid scan measures

A local rank grid scan picks a keyword, such as "dentist", and a set of points around your business, for example a 5 by 5 square covering a few kilometres. For each point it checks where your business appears in the map results as if the search came from that spot. The result is a map of coloured pins: a number for your position at each point, or a mark for "not found".

Read it like a weather map of visibility. Pins near your shop are usually strongest and weaken as you move away. The shape of that fade tells you things a single number cannot:

  • A tight circle of good positions around the shop means you are mostly winning on proximity.
  • A lopsided pattern, strong to the east and weak to the west, often points to a stronger competitor or a barrier such as a river, railway line or flyover that changes who is "nearby".
  • Good positions far from your shop suggest strong relevance and prominence, which is the part you can improve.

A rough average of all points is sometimes shown as an average position or visibility score. Use it as a trend line, not as a grade.

Why positions differ between locations, devices and times

Several things move the numbers even for the same keyword: the searcher's exact location, the device, whether they are logged in, past behaviour, the time of day (for "open now" searches), and ongoing changes in Google's systems. Scan tools sample these conditions, so two scans taken an hour apart can differ a little without anything having changed on your profile.

For this reason, compare like with like. Use the same keyword, grid size and radius each time, scan on a similar day and time, and look at the pattern over several scans, not one.

How this differs from Search Console and your Business Profile numbers

Google Search Console reports performance for your website in Google Search. Its "average position" is the average position of your website's links across all the times they were shown for a query. It does not tell you your Maps position at a given street corner, and it covers only your website, not your Business Profile.

The Business Profile performance data shows how people found your profile: views, calls, direction requests and the search terms that triggered impressions, but it does not give a position for each. So you need three different views: a grid scan for map visibility by location, Search Console for website queries, and profile performance for actions taken. None is "the real rank"; each answers a different question.

How to read results step by step

  • Pick one keyword that a customer would genuinely type, such as "two wheeler service near me" or "gynaecologist in Kothrud".
  • Scan with a radius that matches how far your customers travel: 1 to 2 km for a kirana or salon, more for a hospital or wedding venue.
  • Look at the shape first, then the numbers. Where does visibility fall away, and why?
  • Compare against two or three nearby competitors if your tool allows it, using the same grid.
  • Pick one action to test, such as adding services or getting more recent reviews, and re-scan in a few weeks to see the pattern, not a single point.

The Rank Grid Simulator on LocalBoost is an illustration of how distance and review strength can shape visibility. It does not query Google. Use it to understand the idea, then use your real scan data for decisions.

Limitations you should know

  • Scans are samples, not a census of every search that happens.
  • A tool's "position" depends on its provider, settings and how it sets the search location, so numbers from different tools are not directly comparable.
  • Rank is a means. Calls, direction requests, WhatsApp enquiries and footfall are what matter to the business.
  • Nobody can guarantee a position. Be cautious of any promise of a number one rank.

A worked example (illustrative)

Imagine a two-wheeler service centre in Pune that scans "bike service near me" on a 5 by 5 grid with a 3 km radius. The pins near the workshop show positions 1 to 3, the east side fades to positions 6 to 10, and the west side, past a busy flyover, shows "not found". This is an invented example to show how to think, not data from a real business.

What can the owner do with it? First, notice that the weak side is also where a larger competitor sits. Second, check whether the profile lists the services customers on that side ask about, such as "scooter servicing" or "puncture repair", and whether recent reviews mention those. Third, decide whether serving that side is worth it at all. If the flyover makes a ten-minute ride into forty, a weak pin there may be perfectly fine.

The point of the grid is to turn a vague worry ("I am not showing up") into a specific question ("why am I weak on the west side, and do I want that customer?").

A monthly routine that takes an hour

  • Week 1: run the scans for your three to five keywords and save screenshots or exports with the date.
  • Week 2: read your Business Profile performance: calls, direction requests, website clicks and search terms.
  • Week 3: act on one or two findings, such as adding missing services, uploading fresh photos or answering unanswered reviews.
  • Week 4: note what you did in a simple log so that next month's scan can be explained.

Keep the log short. A table with the date, the keyword, the average position or visibility, and one line about what changed is enough. Over six months that log becomes the most honest report on what works for your business.

What to do when positions drop

A drop is not always bad news. Before reacting, rule out simple causes: a different scan time, a different grid or keyword, a new competitor opening nearby, a temporary change such as hours shown as closed, or a general update to Google's systems that moved many businesses at once. Check that your profile is still verified, your category has not been changed by a suggested edit, and your hours and address are correct.

If the drop persists for several weeks and you cannot find a change on your side, look at what the businesses that now outrank you do differently: more recent reviews, a more specific category, better photos, more complete services. Copy the useful ideas honestly. Never buy reviews or use fake listings to chase a position; these risk suspension and can erase every gain.

Rank-tracking checklist

Common mistakes to avoid

  • Judging rank by searching from your own shop.
  • Tracking only the exact business name, which tells you nothing about discovery.
  • Chasing one pin on the map instead of the overall pattern.
  • Changing the keyword every month and then comparing results.
  • Treating a tool's score as Google's own number.
  • Ignoring conversion: calls and visits matter more than position.

Frequently Asked Questions

Is a local rank tracker accurate?

It is accurate as a sample of what searchers at specific points may see, but it cannot reproduce every personalised result. Use it for direction and trends, and confirm with real actions such as calls and direction requests.

How often should I scan my rankings?

Once a month is enough for most small businesses. Scanning daily mostly shows noise. Scan after a meaningful change such as new categories or a burst of reviews.

Why am I strong near my shop but weak a few kilometres away?

Distance is a major factor in local results, so businesses nearer the searcher get an edge. To widen your strong area, improve relevance and prominence: accurate categories, complete services, steady genuine reviews and a consistent web presence.

Can I see my rank for free?

You can check manually by searching from different places, and use free tools on LocalBoost such as the local SEO checklist to review your basics. Detailed grid scans usually need a provider and are part of paid features.
